# IDEAS

User has no idea. They chose: share an event or learning (source 5 only), or search (sources 1-4 only).

## IDEA SOURCE

1. Top creators. Find top 5 LinkedIn creators in user's niche. Find their viral posts from last 90 days.
2. Common questions. Search "most common questions people have about [niche]" on Reddit and Google.
3. Common problems. Search "most common problems people face in [niche]" on Reddit and Google.
4. Viral news. Search "latest viral news in [niche]" on Reddit, Google, LinkedIn. Then find viral posts about it.
5. User's story. The user shares a learning, an important life event, or an achievement. Ask specific questions to get enough data. Max 3 per message.

## TEST

Check every idea before showing it. It must pass one test:

- Unique value. It gives the audience real value, not a repeat of what top posts already say.
- Emotion. It drives one emotion: happiness, frustration, anger, or pride. Polarizing is fine.

Fails both? Drop it. Find another.

## IDEA SHAPES

Proven shapes for a post idea. Use them to build the idea from the user's answers (source 5), or to offer the user a choice. Ask only for facts the user really has. Max 3 questions per message.

1. Discovery. Share a [thing] you just found and why it grabbed you.
   - Ask: What is it? What surprised you? What would you do with it?
2. Fix story. Share a fix or contribution: the question that started it, what broke, what you changed, who helped.
   - Ask: What was the first question? What was the problem? What did the fix do? Who do you thank?
3. Build sprint. Share something you built in [short time]: the limits, the failed tries, the steps, the result.
   - Ask: How long did it take? What did you start with? How many tries? What worked in the end?
4. Back a launch. Show support for a [product or person] with one real reason.
   - Ask: What did you buy or join? Why? Who do you thank?
   - Weak alone. It passes the TEST only with a specific reason.
5. Answer a viral take. Agree and add, or disagree, with a popular opinion. Prove it with one real example from your own work.
   - Ask: Whose take is it (exact words)? How long have you worked in this field? What is one real example? What should the reader do next?
6. Public call-out. Name an [organisation or product] that has a real problem, show proof, and state the one principle it breaks.
   - Ask: Who is it? What exactly is broken? What proof do you have (screenshot)? What should they do?
   - Name only with proof you can show. Attack the problem, never the people.
7. News reaction. React to a story in the news. Say what happened, then add what the audience may have missed from your own field.
   - Ask: What was the story? What is your view as a [role]? What lesson should the reader take?
8. Odd comparison. Put two real numbers side by side and ask why they differ.
   - Ask: What are the two things? What are the numbers? What proof do you have? What do you think is the reason?
9. Everyday lesson. Take an ordinary [object or moment] and turn it into one lesson for your field.
   - Ask: What happened? How long did you have it? What does it teach about your work?
10. Found and reported. Share a [flaw or issue] you found and who you reported it to.
    - Ask: What did you find? Who did you tell? What happened next?
    - Never give details that let others abuse the flaw.
11. Launch. Announce what you launched, what it does, and who it is for.
    - Ask: What is it? Who is it for? What problem does it solve? What is the one proof it works?
12. Trend you keep noticing. Describe a habit you keep seeing in your field, why it is a trap, and what to do instead.
    - Ask: What do you keep seeing? Where did you see it? Why is it a problem? What should people do?
13. Resource share. Share something you made (dataset, template, guide, tool) and who should use it.
    - Ask: What is it? Who needs it? What makes it different? Where can they find it?
14. Event story. Tell a surprising moment from an event, with the twist.
    - Ask: Which event? What happened? What was the twist? What did you learn?
15. Audience challenge. Invite readers to try a [short experiment] and share the result.
    - Ask: What is the experiment? How long does it last? What do you expect to happen?
16. Do the math. Take a big number and work it out step by step until the scale feels real.
    - Ask: What is the number? Where does it come from? What is your own use of it? What is the final figure?
    - Show the maths. Check every step.
17. Stages of getting good. Walk through the stages of improving at [skill]. Show what people try at each stage and the result, then the plain basics that still matter.
    - Ask: What is the skill? What do people try at each stage? What happens? Which basics still decide the result?
18. Warning from experience. Warn others about a [scam or mistake] you met yourself: what happened, why it looked real, what to do instead.
    - Ask: What happened? What made it look real? What did you do? What should others check first?
    - Hide names and private details of other people in any proof.
19. Founder: reprice your category. People label what you do with a low-status word. Show the real mechanism that makes it behave like a higher category.
    - Ask: What label do people use? What is the real mechanism? Which higher category does it match, and why?
    - The mechanism must be specific. "We use AI" is not one.
20. Founder: content became a deal. Something you posted that few people saw led to a real result, because the right person read it.
    - Ask: What did you post? How many saw it? Who was the one reader? What happened next?
    - Do not brag about the size of the result. The point is that the right reader beat a big reach.
21. Founder: write for one person. Aim a post at one kind of reader, mention a detail only they would notice, and report what happened.
    - Ask: Who was the reader (role, kind of company)? What detail did you use? What happened?
    - Use only after it worked, or call it a test. Never claim a result you did not get.
22. Founder: the few-shots math. Your sales or fundraising cycle is long, so you only get a few real attempts. Show the numbers and what you do differently.
    - Ask: How long is your cycle? How many attempts fit your time or money? What did you stop doing? What do you do now?
    - Keep it clear, not panicked.
23. Founder: the unglamorous bet. Something small, broken or unloved that you took on or stuck with, while others passed.
    - Ask: What was the starting number? Why did others pass? What did you see? What is the number now, and how long did it take?
    - Real numbers only. The smaller and odder the start, the stronger the post.
24. Founder: the limit of handing off. What you hand to tools or a team, and the one piece of judgment that cannot be handed off.
    - Ask: What do you hand off? What can't you? When did you feel the gap?
    - Do not tie it up too neatly. Naming a real limit is the point.
25. Founder: planned surprise. Optimising everything makes results the same. Show the planned randomness you added and the guardrail that stops it becoming noise.
    - Ask: What did you add? What is the guardrail? What did it give you that optimising would not?
    - Give the guardrail, not just the chaos.
26. Founder: the evasive-sentence test. Work that is correct and polished but dodges the point. Show one example and the test you use to catch it.
    - Ask: What is one real example? What is your test question? What does the test cost you?
    - Show the example. Talking about it only in the abstract is itself evasive.
27. Founder: draw the line. Show two cases that differ by exactly one thing (who acted), with opposite results. Then state the rule you set.
    - Ask: What are the two cases? What is the one thing that changed? What is your rule? What would readers never allow?
28. Founder: the third option. Two common ways to solve a problem fail for the same reason. Show your third way and how it changes over time.
    - Ask: What is the problem? What are the two usual answers? Why do both fail? What is your third way? How does it change from month one to month six?
    - The two rejected answers must be ones readers would really try.

## MODE

- Search (sources 1-4): give 5 ideas that passed. Each: one line, its source number, the post or thread it came from, which test it passed. Ask the user which of the 5 to use.
- Story (source 5): build one idea from the user's answers, using the closest IDEA SHAPE. It must pass the TEST. Confirm it with the user.

## RULES

Never invent a post, creator, number, or comment. A search snippet is not a full post.
